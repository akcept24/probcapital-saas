The real cost of a 50% revenue share

"No monthly fee" is the most expensive sentence in prop-firm software. Revenue share sounds like alignment — until you run your own numbers. Here they are, with real published vendor prices and a worked example you can check on a napkin.

By ProbCapital — we run our own firm on this stack.

The pitch sounds fair

Every revenue-share offer opens the same way: you pay little or nothing fixed, the vendor earns only when you earn, interests aligned. For a founder who hasn't sold a challenge yet, that feels like the safe option — no $2,490 leaving the account in a month when you sold almost nothing.

The problem isn't the first month. The problem is that the percentage applies to your gross, forever, and it scales exactly as fast as your marketing works. A flat fee is a cost you outgrow. A revenue share is a partner you can't.

What 50% actually applies to

Not profit. Not what's left after ad spend, refunds, chargebacks and payout costs. Fifty percent of gross challenge revenue — every dollar that comes through your checkout, including challenges you discounted with a promo code to fill a slow week. Run a sale and the vendor still takes half of the smaller number: your margin absorbs the discount, their meter doesn't care.

It also never gets cheaper with loyalty or volume. Month one and month thirty are billed at the same rate, on a number that — if you're doing your job — keeps getting bigger.

The worked example

Example — illustrative volumes, real published prices. Say your firm sells challenges at an average of about $350, a normal mid-market price point. Here is one month, two ways:

The vendor prices behind this table, as published: YourPropFirm's Prop Suite charges a $2,749 setup and no monthly fee, in exchange for 50% of revenue. Our pricing is a $6,000 one-time setup, then $1,490 (Launch), $2,490 (Growth) or $3,990 (Scale) a month — 0% revenue share and no per-trader fees on every plan.

A year of it

Extend the 200-challenge example to twelve months. The revenue-share structure pays out $35,000 × 12 = $420,000 in share, plus the $2,749 setup: $422,749. The flat structure on Growth costs $6,000 + 12 × $2,490 = $35,880, all in. The difference is roughly $387,000 a year. That is not a software bill. That is a co-founder — one who doesn't take your support tickets.

At the 770-challenge level the gap stops being funny altogether: the share alone is $134,750 a month, against a $3,990 flat fee. Success doesn't dilute a revenue share. It compounds it.

Where the break-even sits

A $2,490 flat fee equals a 50% share at $4,980 of monthly gross — about 14 challenges at $350. Almost any firm doing real acquisition crosses that line in its first month of marketing. Below it, you're small enough that the platform fee was never your binding constraint anyway.

That line compares the monthly fees only. Counting both setups over the first year — $6,000 ours, $2,749 theirs — the break-even moves to about $5,500 of monthly gross, roughly 16 challenges; from year two the setups are behind you and the line above is the whole story.

Read that again, because it's the whole article: revenue share is cheapest precisely when it matters least, and most expensive for the entire remaining life of your firm.

"But the vendor only wins when I win"

This is the line that sells revenue share, and it isn't true — because the share is calculated on gross, not on your profit. Your profit is what's left after ad spend, affiliate commissions, refunds, chargebacks and payouts to funded traders. None of those reduce the vendor's cut.

So picture a genuinely bad month: acquisition costs spike, a cohort of strong traders draws heavy payouts, and the firm roughly breaks even — or dips below it. The vendor still takes half of everything that came through the checkout. They won. You didn't. A flat fee, whatever else you think of it, is the same in your best month and your worst; a percentage of gross is a senior claim on your revenue that gets paid before your costs do, in every month, including the ones where there is nothing left for you.

The honest case for a share

To be fair: a percentage can be rational when the vendor genuinely carries something on their side of the ledger — funding your payout liability, carrying real risk, or bringing distribution you cannot buy elsewhere. In that case, price it like what it is: financing. Compare it against your cost of capital, negotiate caps and a buy-out clause, and put a term on it.

But if the offer is the same software, the same support, and the same payment rails moving your own money — with the vendor taking half of gross for hosting it — that's not alignment. That's the most expensive billing method in the industry wearing a friendly name.

Incentives you can feel

A revenue-share vendor makes more money when you sell more challenges at any quality. More refunds, more abusive traders, more payout disputes do not reduce their cut — discount-driven volume increases it. A flat-fee vendor makes the same money whether you sell 200 or 2,000 challenges, so the only way they keep you is uptime, features and support. Decide whose incentives you want sitting inside your P&L.

What flat costs here, in full

Setup: $6,000, once. Launch $1,490/mo, Growth $2,490/mo, Scale $3,990/mo. Brand, domain and hosting included. Zero revenue share, zero per-trader fees. That is the entire price list. There is no second page.

Notice what growth looks like under each model. Outgrow Growth and you move to Scale: a step of $1,500 a month, known in advance, budgeted like rent. Under a revenue share, growth reprices itself automatically — every successful campaign, every good month, raises next month's platform bill by half of the gain. One model charges you for the software. The other charges you for succeeding.
