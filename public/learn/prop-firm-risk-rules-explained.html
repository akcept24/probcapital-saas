Risk rules, explained like an operator

Traders don't hate rules. They hate surprises — limits they couldn't read in advance, numbers they can't check, and blocks nobody will explain. Written the way an operator thinks about them, here is what each standard rule is actually for.

By ProbCapital — we run our own firm on this stack.

Think of your risk rules as the firm's constitution. They decide who gets funded, who gets stopped — and, because every dispute starts with "show me where it says that," whether you win arguments in month three. Six rules do almost all of the work.

The consistency rule

What it does: caps how much of a trader's total profit may come from a single day — in some firms, from a single trade.

Why it exists: a funded account should pay for repeatable trading, not for one lucky spike into a news release. Without a consistency rule, the evaluation selects for the trader who went all-in once and got away with it — exactly the behavior that blows up funded accounts, with your payout pool attached.

Example, with invented numbers: total profit is $2,000 and the consistency cap is 40%. The biggest day may account for at most $800. If a trader's best day is $900, they haven't failed — they haven't finished: they keep trading until the total reaches $2,250, at which point $900 is exactly 40%. The rule stretches performance across days instead of ending the account.

Operator note: publish which variant you run — per-day or per-trade — and show traders their current percentage live in the dashboard. A rule the trader can watch is a rule they plan around instead of a trap they discover.

Daily drawdown

What it does: the maximum an account may lose within a single trading day. On our own platform, the standard setup is a 5% daily limit sitting inside a 10% overall limit.

Why it exists: it's the brake on tilt. Most account blow-ups are not bad strategies; they are one bad afternoon compounding — size up, revenge trade, repeat. A daily limit converts a catastrophic day into a stopped day. It also protects the math of the whole firm: a small minority of traders will happily gamble a simulated account to the wall, and the daily limit is what stands between them and everyone else's payouts.

Operator note: decide whether you measure from balance or from equity, from day-start or from a trailing peak — and write the choice down. Half of all drawdown disputes are really "which number did you measure?" disputes.

Overall (maximum) drawdown

What it does: the total loss budget of the account across its whole life. Breach it, and the evaluation is over.

The design choice that matters is static vs. trailing. Static is measured from the starting balance: simple, predictable, easy to explain in one sentence. Trailing follows the account's peak, locking in gains as they grow — stricter on the trader, kinder to your risk book. Neither is "fairer" in the abstract. What matters is that the trader can see, at any moment, exactly how much room is left: put the remaining buffer on the dashboard in dollars, not in a formula they have to reverse-engineer after a breach.

News trading

What it does: restricts opening or holding trades around scheduled high-impact releases — rate decisions, employment reports and the like.

Why firms restrict it: prices gap through stops at releases, and simulated fills in that chaos flatter strategies that would never survive the same moment live. A trader whose entire edge is "maximum size, one second before the number" isn't trading a strategy; they're buying lottery tickets with your payout pool as the prize fund.

Operator note: the policy must name three things — which releases, what window around them, and what happens inside the window: orders rejected, positions must be closed beforehand, or profits inside the window don't count. Pick one consequence and write it in the rules. Vague news policies — "excessive risk around news may be reviewed" — generate a wildly disproportionate share of disputes, because every trader reads them as permission and every firm reads them as a ban.

Weekend holding

What it does: decides whether positions may stay open across the weekend close.

The trade-off is honest. Markets close; the world doesn't. Monday opens can gap far beyond a Friday stop — and a gap doesn't respect a stop-loss, it jumps over it. Allowing weekend holding means accepting that gap risk into your accounts. Forbidding it means forcing Friday closes and annoying every swing trader you have. Either policy is defensible. Silence is not: the first gap weekend will write the policy for you, in public, in your reviews.

Hedging and copy-trading across accounts

What it does: prohibits running mirrored or opposite strategies across multiple accounts — your own accounts, or a coordinated group's.

Why it's an existential rule, not fine print: go long on one account and short on another, and one of them passes the evaluation by construction. Multiply that across a group and the challenge model inverts — the firm is no longer selecting traders, it's paying out on arithmetic. Copy networks do the same thing in one direction: one strategy, dozens of accounts, dozens of times the exposure to a single idea that you ever intended to fund.

Detection is pattern work: same instruments, same direction, entry times seconds apart, repeated across accounts and days. Treat the group as one scheme, and keep one evidence record for the whole decision — the instruments, the timing, the matched orders. You will need that record the first time a blocked trader tells the story their way.

The principle underneath all of it

Every rule in this article fails the same way when it's enforced as a black box: the trader experiences it as theft, and says so everywhere traders gather. So the operating principle on our stack is simple — every block comes with a reason the trader can see and appeal: the rule, the measured values, the trades involved, the timestamp. The trader sees the same record your risk team sees, and an appeal is reviewed against that same evidence.

It's fairer. It's also self-defense. A trader holding a clear, specific record rarely files a chargeback — and when a dispute does reach your payment provider, the record they read is the one your system wrote at the moment of the decision, not a reconstruction your team assembled under pressure.
