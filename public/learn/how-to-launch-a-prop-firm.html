How to launch a prop firm in 2026: the operator checklist

Launching a prop firm is mostly a sequence of decisions, not a development project. This is the checklist we walk new operators through — and the order matters, because two of the items run on other companies' clocks.

By ProbCapital — we run our own firm on this stack.

The software end of a prop firm is a solved problem — that's the part you license. Launches stall on approvals, unwritten rules and unprepared support. Work through these seven items in order, and the platform itself turns out to be the shortest step.

1. Brand and domain

Pick a name a trader can say out loud, spell after hearing it once, and type into a browser. Buy the domain yourself, in your own registrar account — your firm should never live on an address you don't control.

Then lock the visual identity: logo, colors, tone of voice. It has to survive contact with everything a trader sees — terminal, dashboard, checkout, emails, payout certificates. Traders screenshot inconsistencies and read them as risk. Consistency isn't decoration; it's your cheapest trust signal, and it's much easier to set before launch than to retrofit after your first thousand accounts.

2. Challenge plans and rules

Decide your ladder before anything is configured: account sizes, one-step or two-step evaluations, profit targets, the drawdown model, the profit split, and the fee for each size.

Price from your own economics, not from a competitor's homepage. Your fee has to cover acquisition cost, the traders who will pass and draw payouts, and the platform — with margin left over. And write the rules page now, in plain language a tired person can follow at 1 a.m. Rules written after the first dispute are already too late: every trader who bought before the rewrite owns the old promise.

Two smaller decisions belong in this step, because changing them later always looks like moving the goalposts. First, discounts: decide your promo policy — how often, how deep, who qualifies — before your marketing team invents one under pressure. Second, refunds: state plainly when a challenge fee comes back and when it doesn't. Traders can accept a strict policy they read before paying. What they cannot accept, and will say so publicly, is a policy they meet for the first time in a support ticket.

3. Payments: your PSP, your keys, your money

Open your payment service provider account early. Approvals run on the PSP's clock, not yours, and this step — not the software — is the one that most often decides your launch date.

Structurally, insist on one thing: trader money flows directly to you, through your own PSP keys, into your own accounts. A platform vendor should sell you software, never sit inside your money flow. If a provider's model requires your revenue to pass through their hands — or be shared with them — understand exactly why before you sign. (We did the math on that model in the real cost of a 50% revenue share.)

Before launch, buy your own cheapest challenge with a real card and walk the entire path: payment, confirmation email, account provisioning, and the refund route. If anything in that chain embarrasses you, it will embarrass you at scale.

4. KYC: one provider, chosen on purpose

You need identity verification before money leaves. A payout to an unverified person is a compliance and fraud problem, not a growth tactic. The mainstream choices are Sumsub, Veriff and Shufti; on our stack you pick the provider and hold the contract directly, so the verification data — and the pricing — stay yours.

Decide where the gate sits. Heavy verification at signup costs conversion; verification at the first payout request is the pattern most firms settle on, because that's the moment identity actually matters. Either can be right. What can't be right is deciding later: state the policy in your rules before the first sale, not in a support reply after the first blocked payout.

5. Risk and anti-abuse rules

Set these before the first challenge is sold, because they are far harder to tighten afterwards: the consistency rule, daily and overall drawdown enforcement, your news-trading and weekend-holding policies, and how you treat hedging and copy-trading across accounts. (Each of these is unpacked in risk rules, explained like an operator.)

Enforcement should be automated and server-side — a rule a human checks on Mondays is a rumor, not a rule. And every automated action should produce a reason your team can show the trader: the rule, the measured numbers, the trades involved. That evidence trail is what turns a dispute from a chargeback into a conversation.

6. Support workflow

One channel traders can find, stated response hours, and prepared answers for the ten questions you will get every day: payout timing, rule clarifications, verification status, platform access. Prepare the dispute playbook too — who reviews an appeal, what evidence you can export, how fast you commit to answering.

A prop firm's reputation is mostly its support reputation. Traders forgive a strict rule they understood in advance. They do not forgive silence during a payout question.

One operational habit is worth building from day one: a fixed payout rhythm. Pick a processing schedule you can keep even in your worst week — not your best — publish it, and then never miss it quietly. If a payout will be late, the trader should hear it from you before they have to ask. In this industry, "they pay, and they tell you what's happening" is a complete brand strategy; most of your competitors will fail at the second half.

7. The timeline, honestly

With brand assets ready and your PSP and KYC accounts open, the platform itself goes live in about 7 days: brand and domain setup, challenge plans configured, your PSP and KYC keys connected, and a pilot run with your own team before the first real trader arrives.

That is why the order of this checklist matters. Items 3 and 4 — payments and KYC — run on banks' and providers' clocks. Start those applications first, do the brand and the plans while you wait, and the software will be ready before the approvals are.
